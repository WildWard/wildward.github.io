Bats vs kilowatts

Slowing turbines on risky nights saves most of the bats that would die. What does that cost a wind farm, and what are the bats worth? We ran the numbers with published data, and built an open calculator so you can run them too.

WildWard · 25 October 2026 · 7 min read · companion to Wind is coming to Asia's bat country

The short answer

Protecting bats at a wind farm costs about 1-2% of its electricity. That buys a big cut in bat deaths: 44-93% with simple curtailment1, 74-91% with smart, acoustic curtailment.2

On crop value alone, though, it doesn't pay for itself at a single wind farm. A bat protects roughly a dollar's worth of rice a year, while each bat saved costs the farm hundreds of dollars in lost power. We'd rather show that honestly than sell a number that falls apart.

So the case for bat-safe wind isn't crop dollars. It's a small cost, big financing risk, species we can't replace, and a cost that better data can cut in half.

The United States figure lands close to the published national estimate (over 600,000 bats in 2012, with less wind than today), so the method is in the right range.12 For the Philippines, almost everything is unknown. Most of the planned growth is offshore, where bat deaths have barely been measured: studies find migrating bats far out at sea and some collisions, but no reliable rate.14 The build-out row assumes 2 bats per MW. It could be far lower, or higher on islands and coasts where bats commute and migrate.

Where the numbers come from

  • Bat deaths. North American monitoring puts the median at 3.1 bats per MW per year, higher in forested regions (6-10) and up to about 50 at some ridge-top sites.3 Southeast Asia has no published fatality monitoring yet5, so the default of 5 is a guess. Forested tropical ridges with dozens of bat species could easily be higher.
  • Lost power. Raising the start-up wind speed on high-risk nights cost at most 1% of yearly output in Pennsylvania.1 Across the industry, blanket curtailment typically costs about 2% of yearly output, up to 8%. Smart curtailment, which only slows turbines when detectors hear bats, cut that energy loss by 41-56%.4
  • What a bat is worth to farmers. In Thailand, wrinkle-lipped free-tailed bats were estimated to prevent about 2,900 tonnes of rice losses a year, worth over US$1.2 million. That's roughly $410 a tonne, and the same study's model suggests 300 bats protect almost 700 kg of rice a year.6 That works out to about 2.3 kg of rice, or roughly $1 per bat per year, for rice alone. In North America, bats' pest control is worth $3.7-53 billion a year to agriculture8, and over $1 billion a year to corn worldwide.9 Bats also pollinate durian: a Sulawesi study valued bat pollination at about $117 per hectare per season.10

Why it's still worth it

  • It's small. 1-2% of output, mostly lost on low-wind nights, when turbines make little power anyway.1
  • Financing depends on it. Many Asian wind farms are financed by development banks that follow IFC Performance Standard 6. In critical habitat, it requires a net gain for the species that make the habitat critical, and no net reduction in any Critically Endangered or Endangered population.11 A project that kills an Endangered bat risks its funding, which is worth far more than a percent of output.
  • Some losses can't be bought back. Bats breed slowly, usually one pup a year, so a population hit by turbines recovers over decades, if at all. For threatened or barely known species, every death counts, and no price per bat captures that.
  • The cost falls with better data. Smart curtailment only works if detectors can tell when at-risk bats are flying. In Southeast Asia, no software can identify the region's bats from their calls yet. Better species ID means slowing turbines only when it matters, so less lost power for the same bats saved.

Limits of this calculator

  • Fatality rates and curtailment results come from North America and Europe. Southeast Asian sites may differ a lot, in either direction.
  • The crop value covers one pest on one crop. It leaves out other pests, other crops, pollination, seed dispersal and the bats' offspring, so the real value per bat is higher, but by how much is unknown.
  • It treats every bat the same. One Endangered bat can matter more to a project, legally and ecologically, than hundreds of common ones.
