Field notes · Bats and wind energy

Wind is coming to Asia's bat country

Southeast Asia is about to build a lot of wind turbines, in some of the most bat-rich places on Earth. The world already knows how to keep turbines from killing most bats. But every fix starts with knowing which bats are flying past, and in this region there's no free, ready-to-use tool that can tell you that yet.

WildWard · 25 October 2026 · 8 min read

Turbines kill bats. A lot of them.

Bats are among the animals hit hardest by wind energy. In the United States, one estimate put the toll at more than 600,000 bats in 2012 alone, possibly up to 900,000.1 Other scientists argue that number rests on too few sites to be reliable, and the true figure could be lower or higher.2 In Europe, studies find between 1.5 and 30 dead bats per turbine per year, about 14 on average, which adds up to hundreds of thousands across the continent.3

Those numbers matter more for bats than for most animals. Bats live long and breed slowly, usually one pup a year, so a population can't quickly replace the adults it loses.

Why losing bats costs us

Bats do jobs people rarely see, because they work at night:

  • They protect rice. In Thailand, wrinkle-lipped free-tailed bats eat the white-backed planthopper, a major rice pest. One study estimated they prevent about 2,900 tonnes of rice losses a year, worth over US$1.2 million, or enough rice for around 26,000 people.13 This is one of the species flying at turbine height in Thailand.6
  • They pollinate durian. The cave nectar bat is a key durian pollinator in Southeast Asia. Durian trees far from bat caves set fewer fruit than trees near them.14
  • They're worth billions. In North America, bats' pest control has been valued at more than $3.7 billion a year for farming, possibly far more.15

And many are already in trouble. Worldwide, over a third of the bat species the IUCN has assessed are threatened or too poorly known to assess.16 The giant golden-crowned flying fox, the Philippine bat on our homepage and one of the largest bats on Earth, is listed as Endangered, and one of its subspecies has already been wiped out on Panay island.17 For species that are rare, slow to breed and barely studied, a new source of deaths can tip the balance.

Southeast Asia is a blind spot

Almost everything we know about bats and turbines comes from Europe and North America. A 2025 study in Conservation Biology tried to predict which Southeast Asian bats are most at risk and had to borrow fatality data from other continents, because the region doesn't have enough of its own. The authors call for collision monitoring programmes across the region.5

The first local evidence is starting to come in. In central Thailand, researchers flew bat detectors on a balloon kite up to 100 m, inside the height swept by local turbine blades (21 to 159 m). They found six open-air hunting species working that zone, including the wrinkle-lipped free-tailed bat (Mops plicatus) and two house bats (Scotophilus kuhlii and S. heathii).6 In India, a 2013 study at wind farms in Gujarat found few recorded deaths, and its authors also said far more data was needed.7

With 390+ bat species across Southeast Asia, the region has more bats to lose and less data on them than anywhere turbines have been studied.

And the turbines are coming

The build-out is real. The Philippines has awarded 92 offshore wind service contracts with around 68 GW of potential capacity, and its first offshore auction targets 3.3 GW for delivery from 2028.8 Vietnam had about 4.8 GW of wind installed by the end of 2023, with more going up in the Mekong Delta: Vinh Long province alone runs 650 MW and aims for 922 MW by the end of 2026.910

Clean energy is the right call. The aim is to build it without wiping out the animals that keep forests and farms healthy.

The fixes exist. They need ears.

Wind farms elsewhere have learned to cut bat deaths sharply while losing very little power:

  • Curtailment. Most bats fly in low wind, when turbines make little power anyway. Starting turbines at a slightly higher wind speed cut bat deaths by 44-93% on the nights tested in Pennsylvania, for at most 1% of yearly output.4 Across six North American sites, each extra 1 m/s of start-up wind speed cut deaths by about a third.11
  • Smart curtailment. Detectors on the turbines listen for bats in real time, and the turbines slow down only when bats are actually there. In a randomised trial in Wisconsin, this meant 74-91% fewer deaths of five bat species, and turbines stood still less often than under blanket curtailment.12
  • Better siting. Surveys before construction show where and when bats are most active, so turbines can go up away from roosts and flight paths.6

Every one of these runs on acoustic data: recordings of bat calls, identified to species. In Europe and North America, software does much of that identification. In Southeast Asia that work has barely started. A 2022 study built a semi-automated classifier for Borneo that sorts calls into four call types and names some species,18 but there's no free tool that covers the region and learns local species. When we tested a leading free bat AI on Malaysian recordings, it named none of the 12 bats correctly, because it only knows European species.

Where WildWard comes in

That's the gap we're building for: free, open, offline software that identifies Southeast Asian bats from their calls, good enough for impact surveys and, later, for smart curtailment. Open matters here. A developer, a regulator and an independent researcher can all run the same tool on the same recordings and check each other's results.

On library recordings, our method already names 78% of recordings to the right species across 91 Southeast Asian species, and 90% to the right genus.

Where we honestly are

  • Our accuracy comes from clean, close-range library recordings. Recordings from turbine height are fainter and messier, so real accuracy will be lower until we train on survey data from wind sites.
  • The tool can't yet reliably flag a species it has never heard. We say so in the software and in our results.
  • Our test recordings are licensed for non-commercial research only. Commercial survey work needs training data from partners who agree to it, and we'll only use it with their permission.
